# 0.7 — Ore Deposits 101

This is the heart of exploration: turning a rock into a **resource**. Understand the
vocabulary and the deposit types, and Nigeria's ores start to make sense.

## Key terms

- **Rock** — any solid aggregate of minerals.
- **Mineral** — a naturally occurring inorganic solid with a fixed composition/structure.
- **Ore mineral** — a mineral from which a valuable element can be extracted *profitably*.
- **Ore** — rock enriched enough in the ore mineral to be mined at a profit.
- **Gangue** — the worthless minerals mixed with the ore (quartz, calcite, etc.).
- **Grade** — concentration of the valuable element (e.g. g/t gold, % Sn, % Fe).
- **Cut-off grade** — the minimum grade worth mining; anything below is waste.
- **Tenor** — the grade of the *ore mineral itself* (e.g. % Sn in cassiterite).
- **Tonnage** — the total quantity of ore (and metal) present.
- **Recovery** — the fraction of metal actually extracted in processing.
- **Dilution** — waste rock unavoidably mined with the ore, lowering the feed grade.
- **Strip ratio** — tonnes of waste removed per tonne of ore (open-pit economics).
- **Beneficiation / concentration** — physically upgrading ore (crushing, gravity,
  flotation) before smelting.
- **Concentrate** — the upgraded, metal-rich product of beneficiation.
- **Lode vs placer** — a *lode* is the hard-rock (in-situ) deposit; a *placer* is the
  reworked, water-concentrated version.
- **Stockwork** — a mesh of many small veins through a volume of rock.
- **Refractory ore** — ore that resists normal processing (e.g. gold locked in sulfides).
- **Reserve vs. Resource** — *resource* = estimated metal present; *reserve* = the
  part that's economic to mine now (at current price & technology).

> Whether something is "ore" depends on **price and technology**, not just geology —
> low-grade rock can become ore if the metal price rises or a cheaper process appears.

## Main deposit types (with Nigerian examples)

| Type | How it forms | Nigerian example |
|------|--------------|------------------|
| **Magmatic / pegmatitic** | Minerals crystallize/concentrate from magma | Tin–columbite in **pegmatites** (Jos); chromite in ultramafics; lithium |
| **Hydrothermal (lode/vein)** | Hot fluids deposit minerals in fractures | Vein **gold** in schist belts; **lead-zinc** (Benue); tungsten |
| **Skarn / contact** | Magma reacts with carbonate wall-rock | Tungsten (scheelite), iron, copper, zinc at granite–limestone contacts |
| **Placer** | Dense grains concentrated by water/wind | **Cassiterite & gold** in rivers; heavy-mineral beach sands (Ti, Zr) |
| **Sedimentary / evaporite** | Deposited/evaporated/precipitated in basins | **Ironstone, limestone, coal, salt, gypsum, phosphate** |
| **Residual/lateritic** | Weathering concentrates insoluble minerals | **Lateritic** iron & aluminium (bauxite); some **gold & manganese** |
| **Metamorphic** | Concentrated by heat/pressure/fluids | Some **marble, graphite, kyanite, sillimanite** |
| **Porphyry / VMS** (context) | Magmatic-hydrothermal, large low-grade or massive sulfide | Not major in Nigeria, but useful analogues for Cu |

## Reading alteration & gossans in the field

Ore-forming fluids change the rocks they pass through (**alteration**), leaving
clues at the surface:

- **Gossan** — an iron-rich (limonite/hematite) cap over a sulfide deposit, often the
  rusty, honeycomb "iron hat" at surface (see [Alteration & gossans](../05-field-identification/05-03-alteration-gossans.md)).
- **Boxworks** — the cellular casts left where sulfide grains dissolved out of a gossan.
- **Tourmaline** (black "schorl") and **greisen** (quartz–mica rock) — alteration
  around tin–tungsten granites/pegmatites.
- **Kaolinization** — clays from altered feldspar; a common halo.
- **Silicification** — hard, silica-rich (jasperoid) rock.
- **Alteration zoning** — many deposits show a pattern (e.g. potassic core → phyllic →
  argillic → propylitic outward); mapping the zoning points you toward the ore.

**Pathfinder elements** travel with the ore and are easier to detect: **As and Sb**
(with gold), **Cu, Zn, Pb, Ag** (with base metals), **Sn, W, Li** (with pegmatites).
Assaying pathfinders in soil/rock is a standard trick to find a hidden deposit.

> **Field note:** a rusty gossan or unusual clay near a granite/schist contact is
> worth a geochemical sample every time.

## Grade, cut-off & the value chain (the logic)

Exploration is ultimately an **economic** exercise:

1. **Discover** tonnage × grade (from drilling + assays).
2. Apply a **cut-off grade** (the lowest grade that pays for mining + processing).
3. Subtract dilution and apply **recovery** → **contained, recoverable metal**.
4. Compare revenue (metal × price) to cost → is it a **reserve**?

> A 1 g/t gold deposit can be economic at scale (open-pit, heap-leach), while 1 g/t
> in narrow veins underground usually is not — **context and scale matter as much as grade.**

## The exploration toolkit (recap)

- **Geology mapping** — rock types, structure, alteration.
- **Geochemistry** — soil, rock-chip, and stream-sediment sampling (+ pathfinders).
- **Geophysics** — magnetics, gravity, EM, resistivity, IP, seismic.
- **Drilling & trenching** — test what the above has targeted.

## Tips & facts

- **Tip:** follow the **structure** and the **alteration**; they lead to the ore even
  when the ore itself isn't exposed.
- **Tip:** in lateritic Nigeria, assay **soil and laterite**, not just fresh rock —
  secondary dispersion spreads the signal.
- **Fact:** Nigeria's biggest historical earner from solids was **tin** — a pegmatite
  (magmatic) deposit — while its present giant is **oil** — a sedimentary reservoir.
- **Fact:** a **placer** can be economic at very low grade because nature has already
  done the mining and concentration for you (e.g. alluvial cassiterite).
