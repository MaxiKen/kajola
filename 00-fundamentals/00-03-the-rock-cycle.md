# 0.3 — The Rock Cycle

Rocks are not permanent — they are continuously recycled from one type into
another. The **rock cycle** is the engine that links the three rock families.

## The three processes (in detail)

1. **Melting & cooling (igneous).** Rock melts into **magma** (underground) or
   **lava** (at the surface), then cools and crystallizes into **igneous rock**.
   Slow cooling (deep) → coarse crystals; fast cooling (surface) → fine or glassy.
   *Timescale: days (lava) to millions of years (deep plutons).*
2. **Weathering, erosion, deposition & lithification (sedimentary).** Any exposed
   rock is broken down (physical/chemical), transported by water/wind/ice, **deposited
   in layers**, then compacted and **cemented (lithified)** into **sedimentary rock**.
   *Timescale: thousands to millions of years.*
3. **Heat & pressure — metamorphism.** Any rock buried deep and squeezed/heated
   (without fully melting) recrystallizes into **metamorphic rock**. Add enough heat
   and it melts, returning to magma. *Timescale: millions of years.*

```
            melts ───────────────►  IGNEOUS
              ▲                         │
              │    weathered, eroded,   │
   HEAT &     │    deposited, lithified │
   PRESSURE  └───  SEDIMENTARY ◄────────┘
   (metamorphism)
```

- Magma/lava cools → **igneous** (e.g. granite, basalt).
- Igneous rock weathered and deposited → **sedimentary** (e.g. sandstone, limestone).
- Rock buried & metamorphosed → **metamorphic** (e.g. gneiss, marble).
- Rock melts → back to magma. **The cycle never stops.**

## What drives the cycle: plate tectonics

The cycle is powered by Earth's internal heat and gravity, expressed through
**plate tectonics**: plates collide (building mountains and squeezing rock into
metamorphic belts), pull apart (letting magma rise), and slide past one another
(breaking rock along faults). Nigeria's geology is the record of these motions —
especially the **Pan-African Orogeny** that assembled its Basement, and the
**Cretaceous rifting** that opened the Benue Trough and Chad Basin.

## The cycle in Nigeria (worked examples)

| Transition | Nigerian example |
|------------|------------------|
| Magma cools → igneous | The **Younger Granites** (Jos Plateau) crystallized and concentrated tin & columbite |
| Rock weathers → sediment/placer | Granite weathers to **laterite** and releases **cassiterite/gold** into rivers → **placers** |
| Plants buried → coal | Niger Delta/Enugu swamps → **peat → coal** (the carbon cycle joins the rock cycle) |
| Shells/lime mud → limestone | Marine lime → **limestone** (Ewekoro, Nkalagu) |
| Limestone metamorphosed → marble | Limestone cooked near intrusions → **marble** (Igbeti, Jakura) |
| Deep burial → metamorphic | Basement sediments → **schist & gneiss** of the schist belts |

## Why this matters in Nigeria

Tropical Nigeria **weathers fast** — heavy rain, warm temperatures, and lush
vegetation break rock down rapidly. That means:

- A thick **regolith** (weathered layer) and widespread **laterite** cover much of
  the Basement Complex (see **Part I, §1.11**).
- **Placer deposits** (tin, gold, heavy minerals) form where weathering releases and
  water concentrates dense grains (see **§0.7** and **Part III**).
- Ore minerals are often hidden beneath soil — so exploration here leans heavily on
  **geochemistry and geophysics**, not just surface rock.

## Tips & facts

- **Tip:** if you find a rounded pebble of granite inside sandstone, you're seeing the
  cycle in one hand — an igneous rock recycled into a sedimentary one.
- **Fact:** the same mineral can appear in all three families — **calcite** is in
  limestone (sedimentary), marble (metamorphic), and carbonatite veins (igneous).
- **Fact:** metamorphism is a *solid-state* change — melt the rock and you're back to
  igneous; the line between "high-grade metamorphic" and "igneous" is whether it fully
  melted.
- **Fact:** Nigeria has no active volcanoes today, but it has young volcanic rocks
  (Jos & Biu Plateau basalts) and fossil ones — a reminder the cycle operated here too.
