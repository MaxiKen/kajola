# Rock Salt (Halite)

> **Family:** Sedimentary — chemical (evaporite) · **Composition:** sodium chloride (NaCl)
> **Quick ID:** **Salty taste** + cubic cleavage + soft (2.5) + dissolves in water.

## At a glance

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Colour | Clear, white, grey, or pink |
| Hardness | ~2.5 (soft) |
| Composition | Halite (NaCl) — common salt |
| Key test | **Salty taste** + cubic break + dissolves |
| Solubility | Dissolves readily in water |
| Setting | Evaporite basins (Lake Chad, Awe, Ijero) |

## Composition
**Halite (NaCl)** — common salt — deposited by the evaporation of saline water (seawater or brines) in restricted basins and lakes. Chemically pure **sodium chloride**; impurities give pink, grey, or other colours.

## Origin & classification
Rock salt is a **chemical evaporite** — it precipitates late in the evaporation sequence, after carbonates and gypsum, when brine becomes concentrated enough for **halite** to crystallise. Thick salt beds accumulate in **restricted basins** where evaporation exceeds inflow.

## Texture & sedimentary structures
**Crystalline**, often in **cubic crystals** or granular beds; clear, white, grey, or pink; **soluble in water**. Salt beds may show **layering** and, being soft and mobile, can flow to form **salt domes** and **diapers**.

## Diagnostic field features
- **Salty taste** — the definitive test (taste a fresh surface).
- **Cubic cleavage** — breaks into small cubes.
- **Soft** (hardness ~2.5) and **dissolves** readily in water.
- Often associated with gypsum and other evaporites.

## Identification tests — step by step
1. **Taste:** a fresh surface tastes distinctly **salty** (the definitive test).
2. **Cleavage:** breaks into small **cubes** (cubic cleavage).
3. **Solubility:** dissolves in water (unlike most rocks).
4. **Hardness:** soft (~2.5).
5. **Vs gypsum:** gypsum is not salty and doesn't form cubes; halite is salty and cubic.

## Depositional setting & formation
Rock salt forms where **saline water evaporates** in restricted basins and saline lakes. In Nigeria, **Lake Chad** and other evaporite basins produce salt both by modern evaporation of brine and from ancient buried salt beds. Salt's low density and mobility let it flow into domes — important traps for oil and gas.

## Host states / localities
- **Lake Chad** (Borno) — traditional salt-producing area.
- **Awe** (Nasarawa) — rock-salt deposits.
- **Ijero** (Ekiti) — rock salt.
- Evaporite beds in the **Chad Basin** and elsewhere.

## Associated minerals & rocks
Gypsum, anhydrite, sylvite (potash), other evaporite minerals. Associated rocks: gypsum, limestone, shale (evaporite sequence).

## Economic relevance
- **SALT** — edible salt, industrial chemicals (chlor-alkali), water treatment, and a source of **sodium and chlorine**.
- Lake Chad salt supports local economies and livelihoods.
- **Potash (sylvite)** where present — a fertilizer mineral.
- Salt domes can trap **petroleum**.

## Lookalikes & how to distinguish

| Looks like | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|
| **Gypsum** | Gypsum is not salty and lacks cubic cleavage; halite is salty and cubic. |
| **Calcite** | Calcite fizzes with acid and is harder (3); halite is salty, softer, and dissolves without fizzing. |
| **Sylvite (potash)** | Sylvite tastes bitter-ish (not purely salty) and is more potassium-rich. |

## Field checklist
- [ ] Salty taste?
- [ ] Cubic cleavage (breaks into cubes)?
- [ ] Soft + dissolves in water?
- [ ] In an evaporite basin (with gypsum)?
- [ ] Clear/white/grey/pink colour?

## Field tips
- **Salty taste + cubic break + soft + dissolves** = halite (rock salt).
- Distinguished from gypsum by **taste** (salty vs. not) and from calcite by **solubility and lack of fizz**.
- Salt is **soluble** — it dissolves in wet weather, so look for it in dry exposures.
- Note any **potash (sylvite)** — a valuable fertilizer mineral in evaporite sequences.

## Facts
- Halite is simply **rock salt** (NaCl) — the same chemistry as table salt.
- It is the **last major evaporite** to precipitate (after gypsum) as brine concentrates.
- **Lake Chad** salt production is a long-standing local industry.
- Because salt is soft and mobile, it can flow into **salt domes** that trap oil and gas.
